00 Method

How much of the sky have we covered?

In October 2024, there was a lot of discussion about how many satellites SpaceX was going to put into orbit for the new Starlink constellation, and I started thinking about what this looks like long-term. This led me to mint a new term, which I tweeted about here:

I built this site (with HEAVY help from Claude) to provide an up to date compution of that number from public satellite catalogs every month. Take every tracked object in Earth orbit, place it directly overhead at its own altitude, and add up the patches of sky the objects cover. Divide by the patch the full Moon covers. The result is the TOI, in moons.

There are a couple ways that you could count the occlusion of a given satelite. I present three expressions of it here. The default and probably most accurate is Facing: the area the hardware presents to the ground as it flies. On {{ catalog_date | date_long }} the catalogs held {{ counts.total | int }} objects.

  • Absolute

    {{ toi.absolute | sig3 }} moons

    Each object laid flat and face‑on, showing its largest silhouette. Basically, a worst-case occlusion assuming satellites were always facing the viewer with its largest face.

  • Facing

    {{ toi.facing | sig3 }} moons

    Each object as it faces the ground in flight. Solar arrays that track the Sun count at half their area.

  • Apparent

    {{ toi.apparent | sig3 }} moons

    Trying to account for blurring. Each facing silhouette gets blurred by {{ constants.default_seeing_arcsec | arcsec }} of atmospheric seeing.

01 The unit

One Moon

The unit is the full Moon seen from Earth at its mean distance. Its radius R☾ is 1,737.4 km and its mean distance d☾ is 384,400 km, so its edge sits a small angle α from its center.

α = arcsin(R☾ / d☾) = arcsin(1737.4 km / 384,400 km) = 0.0045198 rad = 0.25896°

The patch of sky inside a cone of half-angle α is a solid angle, measured in steradians. The Moon covers

Ω☾ = 2π (1 − cos α) = 6.4178 × 10⁻⁵ sr

That is one Moon. The whole sky could hold about 195,800 Moons. The Moon’s angular diameter is 0.518°, or 31.1 arcminutes.

An object much smaller than its distance covers a solid angle equal to its area divided by its distance squared. A flat plate of area A seen face‑on from a distance h covers

Ω ≈ A / h²

We use this to calculate the apparent angle of each satellite.

02 The expressions

Three ways to count area

We use the same viewing sum method for all three expressions, ie., each object i sits directly overhead at its own mean altitude hi, ie, the average of its perigee and apogee, and we total up the solid angle.

TOI = (1/Ω☾) · Σ Aᵢ / hᵢ²

We run the sum over all N = {{ counts.total | int }} objects for all three methods. The expressions differ in how we calculate area Ai.

ExpressionArea per objectQuestion it answers
AbsoluteThe largest silhouette the object can present. For flat satellites, the body plus every solar array, face‑on.How much sky would the hardware cover if every piece were laid flat and faced us?
FacingThe orbit-averaged area the object presents to the ground, given how it is pointed.How much sky does the hardware cover as it faces Earth in flight?
ApparentThe facing area grown by the atmosphere’s blur at the object’s distance.How much sky does each satellite appear to touch once the air smears its silhouette?

Absolute

The absolute TOI gives each object its largest silhouette. For a flat satellite that means the body and every solar array, all face‑on to the observer. It answers the question from my earlier post about how large the hardware would look packed flat into one disc.

A Starlink V2 Mini has a 2.7 × 4.1 m body and two 4.1 × 12.8 m solar arrays, 116.03 m² in all. At 480 km it covers

Ω = 116.03 m² / (480,000 m)² = 5.04 × 10⁻¹⁰ sr = 7.85 × 10⁻⁶ moons

About 127,000 of them would cover the Moon. Altitude matters a lot here; viewing angle varies with altitude enters squared, so the same satellite at 350 km counts 1.9 times as much as at 480 km. Summed over every object, the actual altitude comes to

TOI_abs = (1/Ω☾) Σ A_abs/h² = {{ sums.abs_A_h2 | sci }} sr / {{ constants.omega_moon_sr | sci }} sr = {{ toi.absolute | sig4 }} moons

Facing

The facing TOI uses the area each object actually presents to the ground (as best as Claude and I could guess from publicly avaialable data on how satellites are pointed), averaged over its orbit.

  • Flat-panel satellites such as Starlink and most megaconstellations point their broad face at Earth, where the user antennas are. The whole body face counts.
  • Sun-tracking solar arrays turn toward the Sun. Seen from directly below, an array of area A presents A·|cos ψ|, where ψ is the angle between the Sun and the local vertical. Averaged over every Sun direction this comes to one half, so these arrays count at half their area.
  • Earth-facing arrays count in full. AST SpaceMobile’s BlueWalker and BlueBird satellites carry their solar cells on the back of an antenna that faces Earth.
  • Objects of unknown attitude, such as generic boxes, tumbling rocket bodies and debris, use Cauchy’s theorem. A convex body seen from every direction shows on average one quarter of its surface area, S/4.

The one half comes from averaging |cos ψ| over all directions on the sphere:

⟨|cos ψ|⟩ = (1/4π) ∫∫ |cos ψ| sin ψ dψ dφ = ½

For a flat-panel design with a body face a × b and n arrays of L × w, each seen at a factor f (½ for Sun-tracking, 1 for Earth-facing),

A_abs = ab + nLw A_fac = ab + f·nLw

For the V2 Mini that gives

A_fac = 2.7×4.1 + ½×2×12.8×4.1 = 11.07 + 52.48 = 63.55 m²

a little over half its absolute area. Summed over every object,

TOI_fac = (1/Ω☾) Σ A_fac/h² = {{ sums.fac_A_h2 | sci }} sr / {{ constants.omega_moon_sr | sci }} sr = {{ toi.facing | sig4 }} moons

Apparent

Light from space crosses turbulent air before it reaches the ground, and the turbulence smears every point into a small disc. Astronomers call its width the "seeing" and calculate it as a full width at half maximum, θ. At a good mountain site θ is under 1″. At a typical site it is about 2″.

Blurring a shape of a satellite’s area A and perimeter P by a disc of radius ρ adds a strip of width ρ along every edge and a rounded piece at every corner. For a convex shape, we use Steiner’s formula.

A_app = A + Pρ + πρ², ρ = h·θ/2

If every satellite were pushed edge to edge into one disc, only the disc’s outer edge would blur. Spread across the sky, every satellite carries its own blurred rim. The total light blocked stays the same, and that is the facing TOI. Apparent TOI attempts to account for blurring.

Substituting ρ = hθ/2 and dividing by h² splits each object’s apparent solid angle into three terms:

A_app/h² = A_fac/h² + (θ/2)·P_fac/h + π(θ/2)²

Summed over all objects, with Pfac the perimeter of the facing outline,

TOI_app(θ) = (1/Ω☾) · [ Σ A_fac/h² + (θ/2) · Σ P_fac/h + N·π(θ/2)² ]

The first term is the facing TOI. The second adds the blurred rims which falls off only as 1/h, so distant objects weigh more in it than in the area term. The third adds one seeing disc per object, whatever its size or distance. At 2″ each disc is worth 1.15 millionths of a Moon. The data file publishes all three sums, so anyone can recompute the apparent TOI for any seeing.

Who counts

  1. Every object in CelesTrak’s catalog that has not decayed and orbits Earth. Docked modules and visiting vehicles carry their host’s catalog number, so the ISS and Tiangong count once each, as whole stations. GCAT’s records of dockings catch the few joined objects CelesTrak has not marked yet, so a stack always counts once.
  2. Objects GCAT lists in Earth orbit that CelesTrak does not carry, such as batches GCAT cataloged before public orbits appeared. Aliases, placeholders and duplicate entries are left out. When the two catalogs disagree about whether an object has come down, CelesTrak decides.
  3. If CelesTrak cannot be reached, the pipeline falls back to GCAT alone and marks the month as degraded.

This month that gives {{ counts.total | int }} objects: {{ counts.payload | int }} payloads, {{ counts.rocket_body | int }} rocket bodies, {{ counts.debris | int }} pieces of debris and {{ counts.unknown | int }} of unknown type.

Altitude

Each object’s altitude is the mean of its perigee and apogee, h = (perigee + apogee)/2, from CelesTrak. When CelesTrak has no orbit the pipeline uses GCAT’s current orbit, and failing that the median perigee and apogee of objects from the same launch. Objects with no orbit from any source are left out and listed below. Altitudes below 100 km are raised to 100 km.

GCAT gives a length L (the longest body dimension), a diameter D (the second longest), a span across appendages and a free-text shape. Flat-panel satellites are recorded with their thickness as the length, so L < D. The shape picks the body row; rocket bodies always use the cylinder row because they tumble.

The facing area of a box or cylinder of unknown attitude is a quarter of its surface area (Cauchy’s theorem), and its perimeter is π times its mean width (Barbier’s theorem). When the span exceeds the body by more than 5% and the shape mentions a panel, wing or array, the extra length e becomes solar arrays of area e × w, with w the body width; they count in full in Aabs, at one half in Afac, and add 2e to Pfac. Other appendages such as booms and antennas add 0.1 × e × min(L,D), at one half in Afac. When the shape names a tether, the span is the tether’s length and adds nothing.

Objects with no dimensions, which covers almost all debris, use CelesTrak’s radar cross-section as both areas, with the perimeter of a circle of that area. Without that, a default applies: 0.1 m² for debris and unknown objects, 1 m² for payloads and 10 m² for rocket bodies.

05 History

How we got here

The pipeline rebuilds the index for the first of every month back to November 1957, from each object’s launch and decay dates and today’s area model. Objects that have since come down use their early orbits from GCAT. Starlink satellites sit at their original shells until the 2026 lowering. This is a reconstruction, and the chart labels it as one. Each monthly run also records one measured value; those points start in October 2026 and will build up month by month.

When I posted in October 2024, I put the index at about 0.03 moons. The reconstruction gives {{ derived.oct2024_abs | sig2 }} for the absolute TOI and {{ derived.oct2024_fac | sig2 }} for the facing TOI on 1 October 2024. The absolute TOI is now {{ derived.growth_abs | sig2 }} times its October 2024 value, mostly from V2 Mini satellites and the move to lower shells.

Recompute the TOI

Put latest.json and objects.csv in one folder, save this script next to them and run python3 toi_check.py. It needs only the Python standard library. It recomputes the Moon’s solid angle, sums A/h² and P/h over every object, and compares the result with the published values.

toi_check.py
# toi_check.py: recompute the TOI from objects.csv (Python 3, stdlib only)
import csv, json, math

R, D = 1737.4, 384400.0                  # Moon radius, mean distance (km)
moon = 2 * math.pi * (1 - math.cos(math.asin(R / D)))   # one Moon (sr)
half = math.radians(2 / 3600) / 2        # half the 2 arcsec seeing (rad)
n = sa = sf = sp = 0
with open("objects.csv", newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
    for row in csv.DictReader(f):
        h = float(row["alt_km"]) * 1000  # altitude (m)
        n += 1
        sa += float(row["A_abs_m2"]) / h**2
        sf += float(row["A_fac_m2"]) / h**2
        sp += float(row["P_fac_m"]) / h
toi = json.load(open("latest.json", encoding="utf-8"))["toi"]
mine = {"absolute": sa / moon, "facing": sf / moon,
        "apparent": (sf + half * sp + n * math.pi * half**2) / moon}
print(f"{'objects':<9} {n}")
for k, v in mine.items():
    print(f"{k:<9} {v:.6f}   published {toi[k]:.6f}")

Rerun the pipeline

The pipeline is plain Python 3.11 or newer with no dependencies. It downloads the catalogs, rebuilds every file in data/, and runs in under ten seconds once the inputs are on disk.

Shell
git clone https://github.com/isaiahtaylor/toi
cd toi
python3 -m pipeline.run       # download the catalogs, write data/
python3 -m unittest discover -s pipeline/tests

To reproduce a month exactly, download that month’s data release, which holds the input files, and run python3 -m pipeline.run --offline --cache <folder> --out /tmp/check --date {{ catalog_date }}. The output matches the published files apart from the generation time.

08 Limits

Caveats

The TOI is an aggregate area index with a lot of assumptions about geometetry and orientation. My guess is it's 80% accurate mathematically. However, nothing in orbit shows to the naked eye as a dark silhouette. The eye resolves about 60″, and a 30 m Starlink at 480 km spans 13″. At any moment only the objects above your horizon count, and they are farther away than straight up. At any moment the sky covered above one observer is about 1/250 of the absolute TOI.

Assumptions and approximations

  • Assumed array sizes. SpaceX has not published dimensions for the V2 Mini Optimized or the direct-to-cell satellites, which together carry more than half the index. Both are taken equal to the V2 Mini. If the Optimized arrays were 25% smaller, the facing TOI would fall by about 10%.
  • Proxies. Starlink V3 uses the full-size Gen2 satellite from SpaceX’s 2022 FCC filing. Starshield uses the V2 Mini envelope.
  • Classified objects have orbits from amateur observers, carried by GCAT where CelesTrak lists none.
  • GCAT dimensions are guesses for many objects. After curation they carry {{ coverage.gcat_guess_share_abs_final | pct1 }} of the absolute TOI.
  • Inverse-square weighting. Each object counts as if straight overhead. That ranks low objects above high ones by 1/h², which is the point of the index, and is also why it overstates what any one observer sees.
  • No overlaps. Objects never hide one another in the sum. With objects this small and this sparse, that changes nothing at the precision shown.
  • Seeing is one number. The apparent TOI uses a single seeing width for every object. Real seeing varies by site, night and elevation angle.
  • The history is approximate. It applies today’s areas to the past, uses early orbits for objects that have come down, and counts the ISS at full size from its first module in 1998.
